\Title{Asymmetric Relations Between Needs and Data Availability in Predictive Maintenance Systems}
\Author{Jerzy Baranowski\orcidA{}*}

\abstract{Maintenance decisions can remain consequential even when dedicated condition information is persistently weak or unavailable. This paper develops Limited-Data Predictive Maintenance (LD-PdM) as a conceptual characterization of that information and decision regime. Limited data refers here to the structure and relevance of the available evidence, rather than sample count alone. Four asymmetries organize the argument: weak condition observability, sparse and selective failure evidence, constraints on information acquisition, and the coexistence of uncertain individual states with possible population-level planning value. The framework connects proxy, contextual, historical, and prior evidence to explicit beliefs, decision consequences, and information-gathering choices. It distinguishes usage and exposure from degradation, event probability, and maintenance priority, and identifies assumptions required for each transition. A worked qualitative railway-station example illustrates how passenger-flow proxies could inform inspection attention and when conflicting evidence or shared bias prevents a defensible ordering. LD-PdM overlaps with small-data prognostics and health management, virtual sensing, and established probabilistic maintenance decision analysis; its contribution is the operational characterization and synthesis of the regime, rather than a new inference or optimization algorithm. The example establishes conceptual traceability, without claiming empirical validation or improved maintenance performance. Future evaluation should examine decision consequences and sensitivity to evidence assumptions alongside predictive accuracy.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Predictive maintenance (PdM) connects evidence about an asset to decisions about its future operation, inspection, servicing, or replacement. Diagnosis and remaining useful life (RUL) prediction are important components of this process, but the operational purpose is to choose an appropriate action. Reviews describe the development of PdM, machine-learning methods, and their relationship to industrial information infrastructures \citep{Selcuk20171670,Carvalho2019,Cinar2020}. The availability of informative measurements influences which parts of this workflow can be implemented. It does not, however, determine whether a maintenance decision has to be made.

Limited evidence is already a recognized problem in prognostics and health management (PHM). \citet{Li2024SmallDataPHM} review limitations in monitoring quantity and quality, economic and operational barriers, and data-efficient learning. Their scope includes maintenance execution, multimodal records, physical knowledge, fleet information, and uncertainty. The question here is how to characterize maintenance settings in which feasible condition-sensitive channels remain weak and indirect evidence materially carries the reasoning. This characterization complements an established PHM problem rather than introducing scarcity or its persistent causes as new discoveries.

We call this regime \emph{Limited-Data Predictive Maintenance} (LD-PdM). The term describes an evidence structure relative to a maintenance choice, rather than a threshold on dataset size. Consider a population of infrastructure devices whose maintenance affects service continuity, while the feasible information budget provides little dedicated condition monitoring for each device. Passenger flows, event logs, service records, documentation, and engineering knowledge may nevertheless inform hypotheses about usage and maintenance needs. The central asymmetry is that the consequence of a maintenance choice and the availability of informative evidence need not increase together. A useful characterization must explain both the opportunity to use such evidence and the limits of doing so.

This is also adjacent to established decision analysis. \citet{SrinivasanParlikad2013VoCM} assess condition-monitoring value for infrastructure maintenance using a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP), while \citet{MemarzadehPozzi2016VoI} connect information value to inspection, monitoring, and system-level scheduling. Bayesian-network applications in safety, reliability, and risk assessment likewise provide established tools for integrating uncertain evidence \citep{Kabir2019154}. LD-PdM does not introduce belief-based maintenance decisions or information acquisition as new methods. It brings the feasible observation architecture to the foreground and uses it to organize a system-level account of maintenance reasoning from heterogeneous indirect evidence.

The paper makes three conceptual contributions. First, it gives operational inclusion criteria and boundary cases for LD-PdM, organized around four asymmetries in observability, failure evidence, acquisition costs, and scale. Second, it separates the inferential links from proxy evidence to usage, exposure, condition, and future events from the decision rule that determines an action. Third, it develops a worked qualitative station example, including situations where the proposed reasoning cannot justify differentiated attention. The contribution is a problem characterization and modelling framework. No empirical station dataset, numerical benchmark, or new inference algorithm is presented.

Section~\ref{sec:asymmetries} defines the regime and its asymmetries. Section~\ref{sec:inference} describes evidence and belief formation, and Section~\ref{sec:decisions} connects them to maintenance choices. Section~\ref{sec:case} works through a hypothetical station example. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses overlap with existing approaches, limitations, and evaluation questions; Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.

\section{The LD-PdM Regime and Its Asymmetries}
\label{sec:asymmetries}

\subsection{Operational definition and boundary cases}

LD-PdM is defined relative to an asset or population, a maintenance choice, and a planning horizon. It applies when the following conditions hold together:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=1.5em]
\item The maintenance choice has a consequential operational or economic outcome.
\item A condition quantity relevant to that choice remains weakly informed by the feasible set of condition-sensitive channels because of persistent deployment, access, or acquisition constraints.
\item Proxy, contextual, historical, or prior evidence therefore makes a material contribution to the assessment of future maintenance needs.
\end{enumerate}
Here, persistent means that the limitation is expected to remain relevant over the decision horizon. It need not be permanent. The same asset can move between regimes if its instrumentation, data access, operating context, or maintenance objective changes.

The framework requires the limitations of the evidence to remain explicit when assessment is translated into action. This is a modelling requirement, distinct from the conditions identifying the regime: an overconfident implementation does not make its observation constraints disappear. Equally, a missing channel does not establish LD-PdM if other feasible channels already provide sufficient information for the choice. The asymmetry concerns the mismatch between consequential maintenance choices and the information available to support them; the four perspectives below explain how that mismatch arises and what follows from it.

These criteria concern information content, not simply the presence of sensors. A machine may produce abundant operational records while providing weak evidence about a particular degradation mechanism. Conversely, a small dataset may contain a strongly informative condition measurement. Moreover, degradation and RUL are inferred in many sensor-rich systems as well. The distinction is the availability and relevance of condition-sensitive channels, not whether a physical state is literally observed without a model. \subsection{Asymmetry of observability}

The operational importance of a variable does not guarantee an informative measurement channel. A planner may need to anticipate loss of service while observing only traffic, location, occasional alarms, or service interventions. Such records can constrain hypotheses about device usage without identifying condition. Indirect measurement and virtual sensing offer relevant methodological analogies. Process control and engine monitoring provide examples of inference from secondary measurements \citep{Ren2018261,Xin2026}, while infrastructure studies use vehicle or bogie responses to investigate bridge or rail properties \citep{Zhou2025,Burnac2025}. These works demonstrate particular indirect-measurement formulations; they do not establish that passenger-flow data identify station-device degradation. LD-PdM requires each proposed proxy to be connected to the quantity it can actually inform.

\subsection{Asymmetry of failure evidence}

The evidence generated by operation and maintenance can differ from the evidence required to learn failure behaviour. Rare faults provide few examples, operating conditions differ, and intervention changes what is subsequently observed. A replacement before failure may leave a right-censored lifetime; periodic inspection may locate an event only within an interval. Missing work orders are a separate problem. A maintenance intervention also changes the physical state, so it cannot always be represented as an observation ending without consequence.

Statistical work on interval-censored data provides machinery for partially observed event times \citep{Zeng2016253}, but its applicability depends on the observation assumptions. In maintenance, inspections and replacement may be triggered by suspected deterioration, making the observation process informative. Accordingly, absence of a recorded failure should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of healthy condition. A conceptual framework must preserve the distinction between survival, intervention, and missing records; a later quantitative model must justify its treatment of each.

\subsection{Asymmetry of information costs and decision consequences}

The value of a maintenance decision and the cost of improving its evidence are different quantities. The latter includes acquisition and installation, integration with existing systems, commissioning and validation, connectivity and data processing, and the continuing operation and maintenance of the monitoring arrangement. The importance of deployment cost is also recognized in small-data PHM \citep{Li2024SmallDataPHM}. It motivates a conditional applicability statement: LD-PdM is relevant where better dedicated information cannot presently be obtained within the operator's feasible resources and constraints.

This condition must be assessed for the particular asset function and decision. Unit purchase price does not determine criticality, and an elevator or door may have substantial safety, accessibility, or service consequences. Additional monitoring can therefore be justified for a consequential function even in a population of ordinary devices. Equally, a station example does not establish that sensing is generally uneconomic for an asset class. Condition-monitoring value depends on what the information changes in the maintenance policy \citep{SrinivasanParlikad2013VoCM}; existing operational data also have integration and interpretation costs.

\subsection{Asymmetry of scale}

Uncertain individual states can coexist with useful population-level questions: where exposure concentrates, which groups merit inspection, or how limited attention should be distributed. Hierarchical modelling can share information across appropriately related units; Bayesian IoT failure modelling provides one example \citep{Baranowski2022248}. Risk-based and opportunistic maintenance studies illustrate the importance of system consequences and coordinated interventions \citep{Yeter2020,Yang2024}. These are established modelling opportunities, not a guarantee that a larger population makes weak evidence sufficient.

Pooling requires a defensible relationship between units, including their mechanisms, environments, and maintenance histories. Shared routing errors or a common misspecified proxy relationship do not disappear when more assets are included. Heterogeneous asset classes may require different models. Furthermore, estimating total burden and selecting the particular asset requiring action are different tasks: identical evidence and priors do not justify a data-derived individual ordering. The scale asymmetry is therefore an opportunity for decision support under stated assumptions, rather than an automatic reduction of uncertainty.

Taken together, the asymmetries distinguish the information architecture, the observation process, the acquisition constraint, and the level at which a choice is made. Weak relevant information and consequential choices are defining conditions. Rare events and population scale are important complications or opportunities, but need not occur in every LD-PdM setting. Their practical role is to determine what can be inferred, what remains assumed, and which decision claims would require additional evidence.

\section{Evidence and Belief Formation}
\label{sec:inference}

\subsection{Separating observations from maintenance quantities}

A proxy is informative only through a relationship with a specified target. Passenger counts might constrain use of an access route; a device-use model might translate that flow into operating cycles; cycles might inform cumulative exposure. None of these links, by itself, measures damage or establishes a failure probability. Age, maintenance quality, environment, design, and latent defects may alter the relationship between exposure and condition. The evidence may combine operational aggregates, contextual variables, inspection and intervention histories, and engineering documentation. Their roles should remain identifiable. A timetable constrains possible demand; a service record describes an intervention; documentation supplies a mechanism or service requirement. Treating these as interchangeable features obscures the observation assumptions. Context-dependent HVAC diagnostics and virtual-sensing studies provide examples of using alternative signals \citep{Haruehansapong2023,Buznikov20181216}, while maintenance decision systems illustrate heterogeneous information integration \citep{Ma2020}. Their particular data requirements should not be assumed to hold in an unsensored station.

\subsection{An explicit belief state}

Let $E_{1:t}$ denote the evidence available up to decision time $t$, and let $M$ denote the assumed observation and evolution model. For asset $i$, a conceptual belief state can be written as
\begin{equation}
b_{i,t}(u,d,\theta)
=p(U_{i,t}=u,D_{i,t}=d,\Theta=\theta\mid E_{1:t},M),
\label{eq:belief}
\end{equation}
where $U_{i,t}$ represents usage or accumulated exposure, $D_{i,t}$ physical condition, and $\Theta$ uncertain parameters connecting observations, use, and condition. This notation distinguishes evidence from model assumptions. It does not prescribe a distribution, assert that the variables are identifiable, or supply a fitted model. A defensible implementation would specify its likelihoods, evolution assumptions, and update procedure.

If a future event is required for a decision, it must be defined separately. For example, with $T_i^F$ the time of a specified failure event and $\Delta$ the planning horizon,
\begin{equation}
q_{i,t}^{F}(a,\Delta)
=\Pr\!\left(t<T_i^{F}\leq t+\Delta\mid E_{1:t},M,a\right),
\label{eq:event}
\end{equation}
denotes a model-dependent event probability under the specified prospective action $a$, rather than an association conditional on an action selected by an earlier policy. Evaluating it requires a justified transition and event model; a belief about exposure is insufficient. The probability of reaching a service condition would have a different event definition. Failure consequences must then be supplied before either probability can be interpreted as risk.

Bayesian updating supplies established tools for learning model parameters from observations \citep{Straub2015}. Bayesian networks can organize dependencies among uncertain inputs and states, and expert systems can make judgment-based assumptions visible \citep{Kabir2019154,Baraldi2015176}. The choice of representation should follow the evidence and decision requirements. A graph is useful for exposing assumed links; drawing an arrow does not validate the corresponding relationship. Likewise, a structured score represents a belief distribution only if its probabilistic meaning and uncertainty treatment are established.

\subsection{Priors, dependence, and limits of identification}

Engineering knowledge may constrain plausible routing, device-use mechanisms, deterioration sensitivities, or service conditions. Such information belongs in an explicit assumption register with its source and scope. Documentation, expert judgment, and previously observed behaviour need not have equal evidential strength. Multiple records derived from the same original traffic estimate are also not independent confirmations. A model should preserve their dependence rather than treating duplication as increased support.

When observations weakly constrain a parameter, its posterior can remain strongly dependent on the prior or chosen model class. Honest uncertainty representation therefore includes sensitivity to plausible alternative assumptions and identification of links that the available evidence cannot resolve. In LD-PdM, this can mean stopping at an exposure comparison rather than extending the chain to a numerical failure probability. Inspection or a targeted measurement may then be more useful than a more elaborate inference algorithm applied to the unchanged evidence.

\section{Maintenance Choices under Weak Information}
\label{sec:decisions}

\subsection{From beliefs to feasible actions}

Beliefs become decision-relevant through their consequences for feasible actions. At a decision time, the planner should specify the asset function, planning horizon, candidate actions, and constraints. Depending on the task, these may include inspection, a predefined service intervention, or deferment within the applicable maintenance programme. Access windows, staffing, service continuity, and mandatory requirements restrict the feasible set. A ranking without an intended action or horizon remains an assessment product rather than a complete maintenance decision.

Established decision-theoretic notation expresses the connection as
\begin{equation}
a_t^\star\in\underset{a\in\mathcal{A}_t}{\arg\min}\;
\mathbb{E}\!\left[L(a,S^a_{t:t+\Delta})\mid E_{1:t},M\right],
\label{eq:decision}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}_t$ is the set of admissible choices, $S^a_{t:t+\Delta}$ denotes the uncertain system trajectory under choice $a$, and $L$ represents the relevant consequences. These can include intervention effort, disruption, and adverse events. When a choice includes inspection, its evaluation must also include subsequent actions conditional on possible findings; an inspection is not valued as a terminal action with no use of its information. Equation~\ref{eq:decision} specifies what would make a belief useful for a choice; it is not an optimization algorithm or a calibrated objective for the station example. POMDP-based maintenance and information-value analyses develop this connection in quantitative settings \citep{SrinivasanParlikad2013VoCM,MemarzadehPozzi2016VoI}.

For the qualitative example below, this logic becomes a conditional comparison. Suppose, for the mechanism considered, additional exposure makes an actionable inspection finding more plausible, while inspection burden, effectiveness, and the consequences of acting on the finding are comparable. An exposure difference could then support examining one asset first. This ordinal premise is an explicit assumption, not something established by the traffic proxy itself. It does not justify replacement or certify that the other asset is healthy. An action rationale should identify the link on which it depends and what additional finding would change the choice.

\subsection{Inspection and the value of information}

Inspection has an informational role as well as a resource cost. Its value depends on whether possible findings would alter a subsequent feasible action sufficiently to justify the inspection effort and disruption. Greater uncertainty can increase that opportunity, but it can also reflect a quantity irrelevant to the choice. Conversely, an asset with relatively concentrated beliefs can warrant intervention when the consequences and estimated state justify it. Uncertainty alone is therefore neither a universal reason to intervene nor a reason to defer.

Information gathering should be distinguished from changing the asset state. An inspection may update beliefs without repairing anything; servicing may change condition and the relevant future evidence. In a sequential problem, the expected consequences depend on later observations and actions as well as the present choice. Information-value work treats these dependencies explicitly \citep{MemarzadehPozzi2016VoI}. The present framework uses this distinction to clarify possible actions without claiming to solve a full inspection and scheduling policy.

\subsection{Population allocation and robustness}

Across assets, prioritisation also requires a common objective and a resource constraint. Ranking by exposure, by expected consequence, and by expected inspection value can give different orders. Different device classes or service functions may therefore be incomparable under a single undifferentiated score. Coordinated maintenance and infrastructure scheduling involve additional system constraints \citep{Yeter2020,Yang2024,DAriano20191315,Enjavimadar2022}; a proxy-based assessment supplies only part of that planning problem.

A robust qualitative recommendation states the circumstances under which its ordering persists and the circumstances under which it should be withdrawn. Relevant challenges include a changed traffic route, incorrect grouping of assets, inconsistent service histories, and a common bias affecting several proxy estimates. Stable rankings are not necessarily correct rankings. Where the evidence cannot distinguish the relevant choices, the appropriate output is an explicit unresolved comparison or a request for further information, rather than a more precise-looking priority number.

\section{Worked Conceptual Example: A Railway Station}
\label{sec:case}

\subsection{Setting and assumed evidence}

Consider a hypothetical station with a concourse, platform access routes, automatic doors, elevators, and escalators. The example assumes that dedicated condition-sensitive channels are insufficient for the maintenance choice under consideration. It does not assume that instrumentation is absent from every device or economically unjustified for these asset classes. Consequences depend on function and alternatives: an elevator serving an otherwise inaccessible route can require different treatment from one with equivalent accessible alternatives. Operational importance and safety requirements must therefore be assessed for each function, rather than assigned to an entire device class.

For this example, suppose the planner has zone-level passenger counts or ticket-flow summaries, timetables and platform assignments, a station layout, device documentation, and dated inspection and service records. These are assumed inputs to a hypothetical example. The maintenance choice concerns inspection, service, or deferment between the current planning review and the next planned review. Existing mandatory service, safety, and accessibility requirements constrain the feasible actions; weak proxy evidence cannot relax them.

\subsection{From passenger context to conditional beliefs}

Passenger information first informs possible device use through an assumed routing relationship. Counts at an entrance do not identify the route taken by every passenger or the device selected. Timetables and platform assignments can constrain plausible routes, while closures and alternative access paths can change them. Routing uncertainty must remain attached to any resulting device-level usage belief. Ticket-flow and passenger-count summaries may also share an upstream source; their agreement is then weaker corroboration than agreement between independent observations.

Station topology gives this interpretation a system-level structure. A graph of entrances, circulation zones, platform connections, and accessible alternatives can encode which devices could serve a passenger stream without claiming to reconstruct individual trajectories. An outage or planned intervention may redirect that stream and change use of neighbouring assets. Device exposure is consequently conditional on route availability as well as demand. If the planner considers taking a device out of service, the resulting redistribution belongs to the consequences of that choice; the pre-intervention exposure map cannot simply be assumed to remain valid.

The next link is device-specific. For an automatic door, passage demand can inform an opening-cycle estimate only under assumptions about grouped passage, reopening, and control behaviour. For an elevator, passenger demand alone does not determine trips, occupancy, travel distance, or the distribution of loads. For an escalator, a continuously running operating regime differs from a demand-controlled regime: passenger flow and run time describe different aspects of use. Consequently, a common passenger indicator cannot be converted into a common wear score across these mechanisms.

Temporal structure matters for the same reason. Equal passenger totals spread evenly through the day or concentrated around train arrivals need not imply equal device activity. Where one opening serves several passengers, clustering can change the relation between passages and cycles. Where use is continuous, running duration may remain similar despite a change in passenger count. The relevant aggregation interval therefore follows the hypothesized mechanism and decision: a daily total can support a broad exposure discussion while concealing the timing needed to plan inspection without disrupting a concentrated demand period.

Usage may then inform a model of accumulated exposure over a stated interval, conditional on the mechanism being considered. Exposure is an intermediate description of operational burden, not an observation of damage. Connecting it to physical condition requires further assumptions about initial state, maintenance effects, environment, and the applicable degradation mechanism. A service record can modify those beliefs without establishing that the device was restored to an as-new state. Absence of a recorded fault is also compatible with incomplete reporting or earlier preventive intervention. Reaching a documented service condition, discovering a defect at inspection, and losing required function before the next review are different events. \subsection{Inspection, service, and deferment}
\label{sec:outputs}

Suppose the planner must allocate limited inspection opportunities while maintaining the required service arrangements. Compare two automatic doors with sufficiently similar mechanisms, documented histories, and functional consequences for a usage comparison to be meaningful. Assume, for the chosen mechanism and comparable starting conditions, that additional exposure makes a service-relevant inspection finding more plausible. Also assume comparable inspection burden, effectiveness, and consequences of acting on such a finding. If routing evidence consistently assigns greater use to one door, and that contrast remains under plausible routing assumptions, these premises can support inspecting it first. The exposure--finding premise would need checking against inspection records in an operational application. The traffic contrast alone diagnoses neither degradation nor failure probability; an exposure ranking becomes a risk ranking only with additional event and consequence models.

Direct service needs a separate rationale, such as an applicable service requirement or a relevant finding confirmed through inspection. Deferment means retaining the applicable service policy until the next review, subject to existing requirements and reconsideration when evidence changes. It is defensible only if that choice remains acceptable given the possible consequences and available alternatives. Neither a low traffic estimate nor a broad uncertainty interval establishes that deferment is safe. Equally, uncertainty alone does not establish that an intervention is beneficial: an inspection has decision value when its possible findings could change the choice enough to justify its burden.

\subsection{Population interpretation and scope}

At station level, the planner can group tasks by access window or functional area while preserving these limits. Pooling usage evidence requires comparable mechanisms and a justified sharing structure; combining doors, elevators, and escalators merely because they share a zone does not supply one. Aggregated exposure may inform a workload discussion while leaving individual inspection order unresolved. Shared routing errors, reporting practices, and intervention histories can affect an entire group, so increasing the number of assets need not resolve the dominant uncertainty.

A spatial summary can support this discussion without becoming a map of physical damage. For example, the planner could distinguish a group whose exposure is plausibly concentrated on a busy access route from a group with uncertain allocation among alternative routes. This may help organize a local inspection visit or identify where a short counting exercise would clarify the evidence. The summary should retain both the assumed route structure and the origin of its uncertainty. A shaded zone labelled as a hotspot would otherwise invite a stronger condition interpretation than the underlying information supports.

The example makes the proposed evidence-to-action reasoning explicit and identifies cases in which it must stop. Subsequent evaluation would need to test the proxy relationships against independent observations, examine their stability after operational changes, and compare decisions under the same resource and service constraints. The conceptual contribution specifies what such an evaluation would have to connect and distinguish.

\subsection{Communicating the basis of a maintenance choice}

The planner-facing output should distinguish a supported exposure contrast, a comparison sensitive to routing assumptions, and a comparison that the evidence cannot resolve. These are different reasons for attention even when presented in the same work list. An inspection request should state what is suspected, which evidence supports it, and what finding could change the next action. A request for better routing information should identify the disputed link rather than assigning the asset an unexplained high-uncertainty score. Communicating uncertainty in this form makes the model contestable by service personnel who can recognize a changed controller setting, an omitted intervention, or an inaccessible route. The assumption and scenario tables illustrate the content of such an output rather than prescribing a software interface.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Relationship to small-data PHM}

LD-PdM overlaps with the broad scope of small-data PHM. \citet{Li2024SmallDataPHM} discuss maintenance execution in Section~1, uncertainty in Section~4.3.2.2, and multimodal and physics-informed approaches in Sections~6.2--6.3. Accordingly, indirect evidence, prior knowledge, probabilistic treatment, fleet reasoning, and maintenance decisions are not individually distinguishing novelties of the present work.

The difference is one of organizing emphasis. Here the organizing object is the relationship between a specified maintenance choice and its feasible observation architecture. The framework identifies when indirect evidence materially carries that reasoning, separates the assumptions needed to pass from evidence to a maintenance quantity, and specifies when differentiation among assets cannot be justified. These tasks are expressed through the inclusion criteria, the distinction between quantities, and the worked station scenarios. A data-efficient learning method can be used within this characterization; choosing that method does not by itself determine whether the available evidence supports the intended action.

The two perspectives therefore need not partition applications into disjoint categories. A setting with few condition samples may satisfy the LD-PdM criteria for one maintenance choice and fail them for another. Conversely, numerous passenger or operating records may remain weakly informative about the condition quantity relevant to a decision. The common evidential requirement is to justify the relationship between available information and the target. The proposed synthesis makes that relationship explicit at the system and decision level without claiming that it is absent from small-data PHM.

\subsection{Relationship to other maintenance approaches}

Virtual and indirect sensing contribute methods for inferring unavailable quantities from other observations. Their relevance depends on the particular measured quantity and model, as illustrated by the cross-domain examples in Section~\ref{sec:asymmetries}. LD-PdM asks how such estimates, their limitations, service histories, and context participate in maintenance reasoning at system level. It does not claim that indirect sensing generally omits uncertainty, or that existing methods cannot already support maintenance decisions.

Risk-based maintenance supplies consequence-aware objectives, while Bayesian inspection planning and POMDP formulations provide established ways to connect partial observations, actions, and information acquisition \citep{Yeter2020,SrinivasanParlikad2013VoCM,MemarzadehPozzi2016VoI}. The present contribution is the synthesis into explicit inclusion criteria, distinct inferential links, and a worked illustration for heterogeneous indirect evidence. Its value should be judged by whether this organization clarifies applicability and prevents unsupported transitions from available records to claimed maintenance knowledge.

Digital representations can organize asset identity, topology, histories, and beliefs without providing complete condition information. Digital-twin reviews and infrastructure decision frameworks offer relevant integration perspectives \citep{vanDinter2022,Mahmoodian2022,Yu2021}. LD-PdM neither requires a full digital twin nor establishes one through a schematic station graph. The useful function of a representation is to retain the provenance of evidence and assumptions and connect them to the intended decision.

\subsection{What a later evaluation should establish}

The station example establishes the traceability of a proposed argument: what is assumed available, which variable it could inform, and why a candidate action might follow. It provides no test of predictive calibration, industrial savings, or superiority over an existing policy. Those would require empirical observations or a declared simulation model and comparison design. The framework's conceptual conclusions should not be read as measured operational outcomes.

Future evaluation should separate the quality of proxy interpretation from the quality of the resulting choice. Where a quantity is observed for validation, useful checks include whether uncertainty estimates are calibrated and whether proxy relationships transfer across operating contexts. At the decision level, relevant outcomes include consequence-weighted missed events, unnecessary inspection or intervention, and the value of acquired information under equal resources. Ranking stability and predictive accuracy are useful supporting measures, but neither alone establishes decision benefit.

The reason is that a decision can depend on only part of the uncertainty about condition. Several plausible states may favour the same inspection, making exact discrimination among them unnecessary for that choice. Conversely, a small difference near a consequential action boundary may make additional information valuable. The appropriate question is therefore which unresolved distinctions could change an admissible action and its consequences. This explains why decision relevance can coexist with substantial state uncertainty, while also identifying when a coarse belief is insufficient. It is a conditional property of the decision problem, not an assertion that imprecise predictions are generally adequate.

A controlled synthetic experiment could isolate noise, shared bias, or uncertainty in the proxy-to-usage relationship. Its conclusions would remain conditional on the generated environment, and an exposure-based inspection objective would not establish failure reduction. Empirical work would additionally need observation and intervention records adequate to distinguish changing condition from changing recording practice. A calibrated failure model or industrial sensing break-even estimate therefore remains a separate research task.

\subsection{Boundary conditions and failure of the proxy chain}

The framework has several substantive limits. First, indirect evidence may be uninformative about the maintenance mechanism of interest even if it predicts usage. Second, different combinations of deterioration parameters and prior condition can explain the same records, leaving condition weakly identified. Third, an apparent population pattern can reflect common proxy error rather than shared physical burden. Explicit distributions help reveal these limitations but do not remove them.

Fourth, applicability is conditional on the acquisition constraint and the value of the choice. Better dedicated information should be considered when its feasible benefit justifies its lifecycle burden; a model is not justified simply because sensors are absent. If neither the consequence of a decision nor the expected value of available evidence supports the modelling effort, LD-PdM provides a classification of the limitation rather than a reason to construct a new system. Safety, accessibility, and service obligations must be considered for the particular function, independently of unit price.

Finally, context and maintenance policy can change the observation process. Rerouting, refurbishment, different logging practices, or selective inspection can invalidate earlier proxy interpretations. An operational implementation would need a way to detect such changes, revise assumptions, and retain an audit trail. The research agenda is consequently as much about evidence quality, calibration, and communication as about more powerful inference methods.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Limited-Data Predictive Maintenance describes a regime in which consequential maintenance choices coexist with persistently weak feasible condition information, making indirect, contextual, historical, and prior evidence material to the reasoning. The asymmetry concerns the relationship between decision need and information availability. It is not defined by a small sample count alone, and it overlaps with established PHM, indirect sensing, and probabilistic maintenance decision analysis.

The paper has operationalized this characterization through inclusion criteria, boundary cases, and four asymmetries. It has distinguished evidence, usage and exposure, condition, event probability, consequences, and action priority. The worked station example shows how those distinctions organize a conditional inspection rationale and expose situations in which the evidence cannot justify a ranking. Its contribution is conceptual traceability, without empirical validation or demonstrated operational improvement.

The practical implication is to match maintenance expectations to the available observation architecture while keeping the cost and potential benefit of better information open to reconsideration. Future work should evaluate proxy interpretation and maintenance consequences together, identify when shared assumptions dominate the result, and establish when additional inspection or monitoring is worthwhile.

## 2. Summary of the submission

The paper proposes the label "Limited-Data Predictive Maintenance (LD-PdM)" for maintenance choices that meet three conditions:

- the choice has consequential outcomes;
- feasible condition-sensitive channels leave a relevant condition quantity weakly informed;
- proxy, contextual, historical, or prior evidence materially informs the assessment (lines 70–77).

It offers three outputs:

- **A three-row yes/no/unresolved checklist (Table 1).** It mirrors the three conditions.
- **A five-step "assessment procedure" (Section 4.1).** Specify the decision, assess applicability, trace evidence, compare admissible choices, record and act. A fallback to the existing schedule applies when no strict common preference exists across a declared model set.
- **A hypothetical two-door inspection example (Sections 5.3–5.4).** The expected gain of inspecting door *i* is G_i = q_i d_i v_i − k_i (Eq. 3). The probabilities q_i are stipulated as 0.1 + βx_i under two routing interpretations. Table 4 tabulates six parameter points.

Supporting material includes a shared-error variance identity (Eq. 1), two box-and-arrow figures, two conceptual tables, and a comparison table with four neighbouring approaches (Table 5). The paper uses no empirical data. It states repeatedly that its contribution is a "conceptual synthesis" of established methods (lines 62–63, 534–537).

## 3. Required revisions (most to least important)

### 1. **The proposed procedure recommends a strictly dominated action in a large part of its own example**

Step 4 tells the user to "Check separately whether a policy improves on the reference schedule and whether it is preferred to the other alternatives throughout the model set" (lines 243–245). Step 5 then says: "If no common preference is supported, apply the documented admissible fallback" (lines 247–248). In the example, that fallback is retention of π₀ (lines 253–254, 424–425). Step 5 never uses the first half of Step 4.

In the example, x_B = 0.5 under both m₁ and m₂ (line 444), so G_B = 4.5β − 0.1 does not depend on the model (Eq. 6). Whenever β > 1/45, policy π_B therefore has strictly positive gain under every model in the declared set. That means π₀ is strictly dominated by π_B in every model. The procedure nevertheless retains π₀ whenever d_A ≥ c(β). This happens in three of the four daggered rows of Table 4: (β, d_A) = (0.2, 0.9), (0.2, 0.6) and (0.5, 0.9).

I evaluated the rule over the declared rectangle β ∈ [0, 0.5], d_A ∈ [0.25, 0.9], using a uniform 1001 × 1001 grid and exact fractions:

- The rule selects B in 51.4 % of the rectangle, π₀ in 48.6 %, and A in 0 %.
- In **44.1 %** of the rectangle it selects π₀ even though π₀ is strictly dominated by B under every model. The analytical area, ∫ from 1/45 to 1/2 of (0.45 − 0.45/(1+10β)) dβ divided by 0.325, gives the same 0.441.

The manuscript treats this as a "limitation of the adopted fallback" that an operator "should acknowledge" (lines 472–479). It also presents it as a finding in the Abstract ("They also expose a cost of requiring unanimous preference", lines 12–14) and in the Conclusions (lines 613–615). It is not a finding. It is a defect of the procedure that the paper proposes as one of its three contributions. A decision rule that can pick an option dominated in every admissible model fails a basic admissibility requirement.

Established criteria for decisions under a set of models avoid this:

- maxmin expected utility (Gilboa & Schmeidler, *J. Math. Econ.* 18:141–153, 1989, doi:10.1016/0304-4068(89)90018-9);
- the imprecise-probability criteria of maximality, E-admissibility and Γ-maximin (Troffaes, *Int. J. Approx. Reason.* 45:17–29, 2007, doi:10.1016/j.ijar.2006.06.001).

At (0.2, 0.9), Γ-maximin selects B: the worst-case gains are 0.8 for B, 0.53 for A and 0 for π₀. The manuscript mentions "a rule that maximizes a guaranteed gain" (line 477) and then does not adopt it.

**Required:**
- Replace the "strict unanimity, else status quo" rule with a criterion that at least excludes options dominated across the model set, and justify the choice against the literature above.
- Remove the "opportunity cost" claim from the Abstract and the Conclusions as a finding.
- Redo Table 4 and Section 5.4 under the corrected rule.

### 2. **The central evidence-to-decision chain is never exercised**

The paper's stated purpose is to connect "evidence and assumptions to admissible actions" (lines 7–8). Figures 1 and 2 are built around "Probabilistic inference", "Belief updating" and "Uncertainty propagation". "Bayesian inference" is a keyword (line 27).

No inference of any kind is carried out. In the worked example:

- the condition probability is simply stipulated as q_i = 0.1 + βx_i with a free β (line 446);
- the exposures x_i are stipulated point values under each model (line 444);
- the evidence E that appears in Eq. 2 never enters any computation.

The step that the paper says defines LD-PdM, from passenger or ticket counts through routing and device use to condition, is exactly the step that is assumed away. Table 3's caption confirms it: "None of these relationships is estimated or validated in the present paper." The result is a generic two-option inspection comparison that could be written for any asset under any data regime. Nothing in Sections 5.3–5.4 depends on the LD-PdM conditions.

**Required:**
- Instantiate the chain at least once, with a stated generative model: counts → routing shares with uncertainty → cycles → q_i with a posterior distribution, not a point value.
- Show how the decision regions move as evidence is added, for example a dated route record or the "short counting exercise" the paper mentions repeatedly (lines 273–275, 431–433, 500–501) without ever valuing it.
- Compute the value of that counting exercise.
- If this is not done, remove "Bayesian inference" from the keywords and stop describing the procedure as connecting evidence to actions.

### 3. **The analytical "results" restate the assumptions; the mathematics is decorative**

- **Eq. 3 holds by definition.** v_i is defined as "the conditional expected net loss avoided by the service response after a true positive, relative to π₀" (lines 382–384), and false positives are excluded (line 387). R_m(π_i) = R_m(π₀) − (q_i d_i v_i − k_i) is therefore the definition of v_i rewritten.
- **Eq. 4, the "explicit reversal condition" (lines 404–406), is a rearrangement** of q_B d_B v_B > q_A d_A v_A after dividing by q_B d_A v_A.
- **Eq. 5 is the definition of a minimum over a finite set being positive.** The added sentence "The same statement applies to a compact continuous parameter set when the gains are continuous" (lines 415–416) says nothing beyond the extreme-value theorem.
- **The observation that bilinear functions on a rectangle take their extrema at corners (lines 481–483) is textbook.**
- **The headline conclusion follows directly from the assumptions.** The Conclusions state: "In the stated example, lower detection at the more exposed door reverses the inspection order" (lines 611–612). Since G is linear in d, lowering d_A must lower G_A.
- **The routing "reversal" is built into the inputs.** m₂ sets x_A = 0.35 < x_B = 0.5 (line 444), so B leads under m₂ by construction. The paper itself notes that A can never be strictly preferred under both models (lines 460–461). The example therefore has only two possible outputs, B or π₀.
- **The detection asymmetry has no stated basis.** For two doors of the same type, d_A ∈ [0.25, 0.9] against a fixed d_B = 0.9 (line 447) is given no physical or organisational reason. Yet the "reversal by detection differences" in the Abstract (lines 11–12) depends entirely on it.
- **The numerical apparatus is overstated.** The Abstract's "Exact calculations" (line 10) and the "deterministic Python calculation from a single input file" (lines 487–488) are used for six rows of arithmetic, which I reproduced exactly.

**Required:**
- Present Eqs. 3–5 for what they are: standard expected-loss bookkeeping, which the paper partly concedes at lines 534–535.
- Remove claims of "analytical results" and "exact calculations" from the Abstract and Conclusions, or replace them with analysis that is not implied by construction (see item 2).
- Justify the detection asymmetry physically, or drop the claim built on it.

### 4. **The "operational definition" is not operational, and the claimed novelty and usability are not demonstrated**

**(a) Condition (1) does not discriminate.** "The maintenance choice has a consequential operational or economic outcome" (line 72) is satisfied by almost every maintenance choice.

**(b) Condition (2) has no measure or threshold.** "Weakly informed" (line 73) is never quantified. Lines 101–103 rule out information value as a measure: "Low net information value is also not a measure of weak observability". No alternative is offered.

**(c) Condition (3) contradicts its own status as a condition.** It reads "Proxy, contextual, historical, or prior evidence *therefore* makes a material contribution" (lines 76–77). If it follows from (2), it is not an independent condition. If it does not, "therefore" is wrong.

**(d) The criterion slides between information and economics.** Line 138–139 makes LD-PdM relevant "where better dedicated information cannot presently be obtained within the operator's feasible resources", which is an economic criterion. That sits uneasily with lines 101–103.

**(e) The name contradicts the definition.** The Abstract states that "The checklist separates weak condition information from small sample count" (lines 6–7). Yet the regime is named "*Limited-Data*", and Table 1 has no row about sample count. The distinction appears only in prose (lines 91–92) and in Table 5.

**(f) The checklist's power to discriminate is never shown.** The only full application is a hypothetical stipulated to pass: "Each answer depends on a stated premise of the example" (lines 308–312).

**(g) The novelty rests on an untested claim of usability.** The manuscript concedes its ingredients are established; "established" occurs 9 times. It states: "Its expected-loss algebra and sign comparison are applications of established decision analysis. The contribution rests on whether the checklist and procedure make this assessment usable" (lines 534–537). Usability is never evaluated: there is no practitioner study, no retrospective case, and no comparison with what planners do now. By the author's own criterion, the contribution is undemonstrated.

**(h) The gap with respect to prior work is not stated.** The paper acknowledges that Li et al. [4] cover "economic and operational barriers", "maintenance execution, multimodal records, physical knowledge, fleet information, and uncertainty" (lines 33–35), then moves directly to "The question here is…" (line 36) without saying what [4] leaves open. Reference [14] has the same problem. Its abstract, which I retrieved via Crossref, says it "employed contextual factors as an indirect measurement" because installing physical sensors "may also require costs and maintenance". That is the present paper's thesis. It is cited only as an "illustration" (lines 176–178).

**Required:**
- Give a quantitative or at least testable criterion for "weakly informed" and "material". Candidates include posterior variance of the decision-relevant quantity, mutual information between feasible channels and that quantity, or expected value of perfect information relative to the cost of acquiring it.
- Remove the "therefore" problem in condition (3).
- Reconcile the name with the definition, or rename.
- State the gap relative to [4] and [14] explicitly.
- Provide at least one application where the checklist outcome was not fixed in advance.

## 5. Recommendation

**Reject.**

The single analytical construction (Eqs. 3–6) is standard expected-loss bookkeeping whose "results" follow from the stipulated inputs. The evidence-to-decision chain that defines the proposed regime is never instantiated. The proposed five-step procedure selects an option strictly dominated under every declared model in about 44 % of its own parameter space, and the manuscript presents this defect as a finding.
